Crowdsourcing Community Input

By Norah Smith

A practical guide for neighborhood associations, planning departments, community development corporations, schools and congregations that want residents to share ideas online

What crowdsourcing is

Crowdsourcing is a way for an institution to hand a task its own employees once performed to a large, undefined network of people through an open call. Brabham (2009), drawing on Howe, names the open call and the large network as the two essential requirements. In planning, an organization poses a problem, residents propose solutions online, and other residents comment on and vote for the strongest.

The method works through aggregation: many independent contributions are combined into what Brabham calls "crowd wisdom." Anonymity reduces the influence of body language, identity politics and unequal power (Brabham, 2009). Cheng (2013) found that online discussion reached a much broader group than the direct stakeholders. The IAP2 Toolbox (2006) groups techniques into sharing information, compiling input and feedback, and bringing people together; crowdsourcing fits the second group, though the toolbox does not name it. The sponsoring organization still decides the question and how results are used.

How to run a crowdsourcing process

  1. Frame the problem and release the data

    Before launch, write the question so a first-time participant can understand it, and post the maps, zoning rules, traffic counts and budget. Brabham (2009) argues that a problem can be crowdsourced when it is clearly framed and its data are available.

  2. Decide what the input will change

    Announce in the call how much weight the results will carry. Options range from building the top-voted proposal to treating the top few as advice (Brabham, 2009).

  3. Build a simple site with clear ways to contribute

    Choose a tool that fits the question such as a pin map, a proposal and voting platform such as Consul, or a drag and drop template of streets and parks. Test it with users and add safeguards such as address verification.

  4. Launch, promote and moderate

    While the call is open, promote it through local press, community groups and public computer sites. Brabham (2009) warns that an empty site looks like a "ghost town," so fill it with clearly labeled examples. Moderate by published standards and let residents comment on and vote for each other's proposals.

  5. Review the ideas and report back

    After the call closes, staff compare the leading proposals against budget, law and technical analysis, then publish what was adopted and why.

Resources and timeline

The City of San Diego estimates that a simple community map needs one to two staff and larger projects need three to four. Budget as well for accessibility testing, promotion and public computers with trained helpers (Brabham, 2009). The readings give no overall timeline, so about three months is a planning estimate.

How it compares with meetings and workshops

Who can take part?Anyone with internet access, at any time, including people who are uneasy speaking in public (Brabham, 2009).People who can attend at a set time and place.

Common challenges and how to address them

Accessibility and the digital divide

Some residents lack devices, reliable connections or digital skills, and sites must work for people with disabilities. Offer public computers with trained helpers and hold an in-person session too (Brabham, 2009).

The cost of web design

Web design and testing can be expensive. Beginning with an open-source tool such as Consul and reusing the design later lowers the cost (Brabham, 2009).

Marketing the call

Begin promotion before launch through local press, neighborhood groups, schools and congregations.

Deciding who counts, and handling resistance

Anyone online can weigh in, including people who oppose the project, whom Howe calls "crowdslappers." Brabham (2009) recommends verifying addresses, weighting votes by distance from the site and using peer ratings and community standards rather than censorship.

Polarization and emotional bias

In Chongqing, 96.3% of 2,500 sampled blog messages backed the "nail household," a family that refused to relocate. Cheng (2013) attributes this support more to frustration with developers than to careful analysis. Asking for a reason with each vote and keeping a staff review can reduce this bias.

Examples in practice

What happened
Residents of Madrid propose, discuss and vote on ideas on this platform, which has more than 400,000 registered users (Ravani, n.d.).
What it shows
A large crowd can be reached, but few budgeting proposals have been carried out (Ravani, n.d.), and the platform's design led residents to compete for votes (Menendez-Blanco & Bjørn, 2022).

What happened
In 2009, a Federal Transit Administration pilot in Salt Lake City asked residents to submit and vote on bus shelter designs, and the winning design was "Sugarhouse Lounge." Participants called it generally effective but criticized the public vote (Brabham, 2010).

What happened
In Xiamen in 2007, an expert's proposal against a chemical plant was ignored until it spread anonymously online, and the mayor promised that no such plant would be built nearby. In Chongqing, online sympathy for a household that refused to relocate helped it win compensation several times larger than what about 280 neighbors received (Cheng, 2013).
What it shows
Anonymous posting gave ordinary residents a voice that official channels had not, but public sympathy for one household can outweigh the interests of its neighbors.

How the setting and planning topic change the results

The planning topic

Small, well-defined questions such as a park or bus shelter suit crowdsourcing because the problem and its data are easy to share (Brabham, 2009).

Growth and land use involve trade-offs that an online call handles poorly, so facilitated exercises such as Reality Check are a better fit (Chakraborty, 2011).

Property and compensation disputes can attract sympathetic outsiders, as in Chongqing, so residency checks matter (Cheng, 2013).

The political setting

Where official participation is weak, as in China, anonymous online voices can be one of the few ways for residents to be heard (Cheng, 2013).

Where public hearings are already strong, crowdsourcing works best as a supplement to them (Brabham, 2009).
